The 50% question

Section 44ADA is a bargain with the tax department: declare half your gross receipts as profit, and nobody asks to see your books. Whether it is a good bargain depends entirely on one thing — whether your real expenses are above or below 50% of receipts.

Most solo professionals spend well under half. A consultant, designer or developer working from home with a laptop and some software subscriptions might spend 15-25% of receipts. For them 44ADA is straightforwardly better: it deducts 50% regardless, so they are taxed on less than they actually earned, and they skip bookkeeping entirely.

It flips when you have real costs — subcontractors, studio rent, equipment, heavy travel. At 60% expenses, declaring a presumptive 50% profit means paying tax on money you never kept.

The limit has two levels, and cash decides which

The receipts ceiling is ₹75 lakh if cash receipts are 5% or less of your total, and ₹50 lakh otherwise. For most freelancers paid by bank transfer or UPI the higher limit applies automatically — but taking a meaningful share in cash silently drops your ceiling by ₹25 lakh, which is a large penalty for an administrative habit.

GST: the ₹40 lakh trap

The threshold for services is ₹20 lakh, not ₹40 lakh. The ₹40 lakh figure gets quoted constantly and applies only to goods. Freelancers who cross ₹20 lakh believing they have another ₹20 lakh of headroom are registering late, which means interest and penalty on tax that should have been collected.

Two further traps worth knowing: the threshold is ₹10 lakh in Manipur, Mizoram, Nagaland and Tripura; and exporting services to overseas clients has its own rules, where registration can be required regardless of turnover in order to claim zero-rating through an LUT. If most of your clients are abroad, take advice before assuming you are under the line.

Advance tax, and the one real simplification

Freelancers have no employer deducting TDS on their behalf, so the liability falls on you to pay ahead. Regular taxpayers pay in four instalments: 15% by 15 June, 45% cumulative by 15 September, 75% by 15 December, 100% by 15 March.

Filing under 44ADA collapses that into one payment by 15 March. That is a genuine cash-flow advantage on top of the bookkeeping relief, and it is the part of the scheme least often mentioned. Miss the deadlines and interest accrues under Sections 234B and 234C — modest per month, but avoidable.

The regime choice is stickier for you

Salaried people pick a regime afresh every year. Anyone with business or professional income gets one opt-out and one return, after which the choice is locked. That makes it worth modelling properly rather than defaulting, and worth revisiting before you commit — the regime optimiser runs both against your actual deductions.

One last thing that catches new freelancers: clients frequently deduct TDS at 10% under Section 194J before paying you. That is not an extra tax, it is an advance against the bill computed here — you claim it back when filing. Check Form 26AS so you are not paying the same liability twice.

What if I also have salaried income?

If you also draw a salary. Freelancers with a salaried job alongside their practice keep the salaried exemptions on that portion of income, and HRA is usually the largest of them. Work the exempt amount out with the HRA calculator and carry the result into the figures above, because the old regime only makes sense once exemptions like this are counted.
