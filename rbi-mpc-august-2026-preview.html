TallyBench / Articles / RBI MPC August 2026
// INDIA RATES

Both forecasts finally moved the right way. The RBI held anyway.

On 5 August the Monetary Policy Committee held the repo rate at 5.25% for a fourth consecutive meeting and kept the stance neutral. It also reversed both of June’s awkward revisions, cutting FY27 inflation to 5.0% and lifting FY27 growth to 6.7%. Better numbers, same decision — and there is a reason for that.

By TallyBench Team · July 29, 2026 · Updated August 9, 2026 · 7 min read

The Reserve Bank’s Monetary Policy Committee announced its decision on 5 August 2026. The repo rate stays at 5.25%, unchanged for a fourth meeting running, and the stance stays neutral. The standing deposit facility remains at 5.00%; the marginal standing facility and the bank rate remain at 5.50%.

The interesting part is not the rate. It is that the committee improved both of its headline forecasts and still did nothing. FY27 CPI inflation was trimmed to 5.0% from 5.1%, and FY27 real GDP growth was nudged up to 6.7% from 6.6% — undoing, in each direction, the pair of adverse revisions that made June’s meeting so awkward.

Why better forecasts did not produce a cut: the June problem was that inflation and growth were moving against each other, so any action would worsen one of them. August removed that bind — and revealed a second one. Inflation at 5.0% is still well above the 4% target, and the RBI expects it to rise further before it falls, peaking in the third quarter. Cutting into the teeth of a forecast peak would be difficult to defend. Growth at 6.7%, meanwhile, is strong enough that nothing forces the committee’s hand. Good news on both counts, and still no reason to move.

Governor Sanjay Malhotra framed the hold as waiting for greater clarity on the inflation path rather than as a judgement that policy is right where it is. His substantive point was that the price rises showing up in the data remain concentrated in food and fuel, without much sign of spreading into the wider economy — and inflation that has not generalised is the kind a central bank can afford to look through. Asked about direction, he described the committee as neither dovish nor hawkish, which is the verbal form of the neutral stance.

The numbers the committee published

The quarterly path matters more than the annual average, because it shows the committee expects conditions to get worse before they get better. That shape is the single best explanation for why the rate did not move.

CPI inflation—4.7%5.9%5.5%5.0%
Real GDP growth7.0%6.4%6.5%6.8%6.7%

Inflation is projected to climb from 4.7% in Q2 to 5.9% in Q3 before easing to 5.5% in Q4, and to 5.3% in the first quarter of FY 2027-28. Core inflation, which strips out food and fuel and is the better read on whether price pressure has become entrenched, is projected at 4.3% — meaningfully below the headline, and the statistical backing for the argument that this is a supply story rather than a demand one.

What is pushing inflation up

The risks the RBI flagged in August were tensions in West Asia, volatility in crude, an uneven monsoon linked to El Niño, and continuing uncertainty in global trade. Note what these have in common: they are supply-side. That distinction matters more than it sounds, and it is why the 4.3% core projection sits so far below the 5.0% headline.

Interest rates work by cooling demand. They make borrowing dearer, which slows spending and investment, which eases price pressure. But if prices are rising because oil is expensive and shipping is disrupted, raising rates does very little to the cause — it just adds a demand shock on top of a supply shock. A central bank facing supply-driven inflation with an already-weakening growth outlook has genuinely poor options, and usually the least bad one is to wait.

What "neutral" is actually telling you

The stance is the RBI's forward guidance, and it is more informative than the rate itself. Accommodative signals a bias toward cutting. Withdrawal of accommodation signals tightening. Neutral explicitly signals that the next move could go either way and will be decided by incoming data.

For anyone with a floating-rate loan, neutral is the stance that argues against betting on cuts. If the committee thought cuts were coming it would be signalling accommodative to start moving expectations early, because guidance transmits faster than rate changes do.

How a repo change actually reaches your EMI

Since October 2019 the RBI has required banks to link new floating-rate retail loans to an external benchmark, usually the repo rate itself. This is the EBLR regime, and it made transmission far faster and more visible than the older MCLR system — but not instant.

Your rate resets on your loan's own reset date, typically quarterly. So a cut does not reach every borrower in the month it is announced; it reaches you at your next reset, which could be up to three months later. Older loans still on MCLR, or on the pre-2019 base rate, transmit slower still and often incompletely — if your home loan predates October 2019 and you have never asked to be moved to EBLR, that is worth a phone call regardless of what the committee does next.

There is also a choice most borrowers do not realise they have. When the rate changes, banks default to adjusting your tenure rather than your EMI. Your monthly outgo stays the same and the loan gets shorter or longer. You can usually ask for the opposite. Which is better depends on whether you want monthly relief now or a shorter loan overall.

What a cut would be worth

On a ₹50 lakh home loan over 20 years, moving from 8.5% to 8.25% — a 25 basis point cut, fully passed through — changes the EMI from ₹43,391 to ₹42,603. That is ₹788 a month, and about ₹1.89 lakh across the full term if you keep the tenure unchanged.

Useful, and smaller than most people expect from a rate cut headline. It is also worth noting the asymmetry: on that same loan, a ₹5,000 monthly prepayment saves considerably more than a 25 bps cut does, and it is entirely within your control. Rate decisions are worth understanding; they are not worth waiting for.

Deposits move too, and usually faster

Rate coverage is written almost entirely for borrowers, which quietly misleads savers. Banks reprice deposits quickly when the repo falls — often faster than they reprice loans. If you hold fixed deposits and the committee signals an easing cycle, locking in a longer tenure before rates fall is the corresponding move.

For retirees and anyone living on interest income, a cutting cycle is a reduction in income, not a benefit. That is worth planning around rather than discovering at renewal.

What to watch on 7 October

The next MPC meeting runs from 5 to 7 October 2026, with the decision on the final day. It lands awkwardly: right around the Q3 inflation peak the RBI has just forecast. Four things will tell you more than the rate itself.

  • Whether Q3 inflation actually peaks near 5.9%. This is the load-bearing assumption in the whole August decision. If the print comes in materially above it, the hold stops looking like patience and starts looking like a policy error. If it comes in below, the case for a December cut builds quickly.
  • Core inflation, not the headline. Projected at 4.3%. If core starts climbing toward the headline, the “it has not generalised” argument collapses and the committee loses its reason to look through the food and fuel spike.
  • The vote split. August was unanimous. A 5-1 or 4-2 with dissents for a cut would signal the committee shifting well before the rate does.
  • The stance. A move from neutral to accommodative is the real tell — it normally precedes the first cut, because guidance moves expectations faster than a rate change moves loan books.

What is the RBI repo rate right now?

The repo rate is 5.25%. The Monetary Policy Committee held it there on 5 August 2026 — a fourth consecutive hold — and kept the policy stance at neutral. The standing deposit facility rate is 5.00%, and the marginal standing facility and bank rate are both 5.50%. The next MPC meeting runs from 5 to 7 October 2026, with the decision announced on the final day.

Did the RBI cut rates in August 2026?

No. The committee held at 5.25% and kept the stance neutral, even though it improved both of its headline forecasts at the same meeting — FY27 inflation was trimmed to 5.0% from 5.1%, and FY27 growth was raised to 6.7% from 6.6%. Governor Sanjay Malhotra said the committee wanted greater clarity on the inflation path first. The clearest reason sits in the quarterly projections: inflation is expected to rise to about 5.9% in the third quarter before easing, and cutting immediately ahead of a forecast peak is a difficult decision to justify.

Why did the RBI raise its inflation forecast and cut growth back in June?

Because the pressures identified were largely supply-side — West Asia conflict, fuel prices, supply-chain disruption and monsoon risk. Supply shocks push prices up while simultaneously dragging on output, so both forecasts can move adversely together. It is the hardest situation for a central bank: raising rates does little about supply-driven inflation and would worsen the growth problem, while cutting would add demand pressure to already-elevated prices. August partly unwound that bind, with inflation revised down and growth revised up, but did not produce a cut.

How quickly does a repo rate cut reach my home loan EMI?

Not immediately. Floating-rate retail loans issued since October 2019 are linked to an external benchmark, usually the repo rate, but your rate changes on your loan’s own reset date — typically quarterly. So a cut, whenever the committee delivers one, might reach you any time within the following three months. Older loans on MCLR or the pre-2019 base rate transmit more slowly and often incompletely; if your loan predates October 2019, ask your bank about moving to an EBLR-linked rate.

How much would a 0.25% rate cut actually save me?

Less than most headlines imply. On a ₹50 lakh home loan over 20 years, going from 8.5% to 8.25% takes the EMI from ₹43,391 to ₹42,603 — about ₹788 a month, and roughly ₹1.89 lakh over the full term if the tenure is held constant. For comparison, a modest voluntary prepayment each month saves considerably more, and does not depend on a committee decision.

Should I wait for rates to fall before taking a home loan?

Timing a rate cycle is difficult and the stance is currently neutral, which explicitly means the next move could go either way. A floating-rate loan captures cuts automatically when they come, so waiting mainly costs you rent and delays equity building. The more useful questions are whether the EMI fits comfortably within your income now, and whether your loan is on the fastest-transmitting benchmark available.

What does a "neutral" policy stance mean?

It is forward guidance saying the committee has no bias in either direction and the next move depends on incoming data. Accommodative signals a bias toward cutting; withdrawal of accommodation signals tightening. Neutral is a deliberate refusal to pre-commit. For borrowers it argues against planning around imminent cuts — if the RBI expected to cut, it would generally shift to accommodative first, because guidance moves expectations faster than rate changes move loan books.
