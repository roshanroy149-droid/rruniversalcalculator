TallyBench / Articles / ITR Deadlines Split 2026
// INDIA TAX UPDATE

The ITR calendar split this year. Your form no longer tells you your deadline.

From AY 2026-27, non-audit ITR-3 and ITR-4 filers have until 31 August. Salaried ITR-1 and ITR-2 filers still face 31 July — this Friday. This is a permanent amendment to Section 139(1), not the annual extension everyone has learned to wait for.

By TallyBench Team · July 29, 2026 · 7 min read

India's filing calendar used to be simple: if your accounts were not liable to audit, you filed by 31 July. The Finance Act, 2026 amended Section 139(1) to give non-audit filers of ITR-3 and ITR-4 a due date of 31 August instead. Salaried filers on ITR-1 and ITR-2 kept 31 July.

The short version: If you file ITR-1 or ITR-2, your deadline is 31 July 2026 and it is two days away. If you file ITR-3 or ITR-4 and your accounts are not liable to audit under Section 44AB, you have until 31 August 2026. This is a statutory change, not a departmental circular — so do not wait for the extension announcement that usually arrives in late July. It already happened, and it did not include you if you are salaried.

The deadline is not set by your ITR form

This is the part that catches people, and it caught tax professionals in the first season under the new rule. The determining factor is not which return form you file and it is not the nature of your income. It is whether your accounts are liable to audit under Section 44AB.

Two people filing the identical ITR-3 can now have different due dates. A consultant with ₹40 lakh of professional receipts is not liable to audit and files by 31 August. A consultant who crosses the audit threshold, or who opts out of the presumptive scheme after having opted in, is liable to audit and files by 31 October. Same form, ten weeks apart.

The practical instruction is to establish your audit position first, and read the deadline off that — not off the form number in your filing utility.

The full calendar for AY 2026-27

WhoDue date
ITR-1 and ITR-2 — salaried individuals and HUFs, no audit31 July 2026

The revised-return window quietly got three months longer

Less discussed than the August change, and more useful. The window to file a revised return under Section 139(5) now runs to the end of the assessment year — 31 March 2027 — against the earlier 31 December cut-off.

That matters because of when information actually arrives. Capital gains statements get restated, an AIS entry turns out to be wrong, a client issues a corrected TDS certificate in February. Under the old rule those discoveries after December meant an updated return with additional tax; now they can be handled as an ordinary revision.

What filing late actually costs

Two charges, and they are separate.

Section 234F is a flat late fee: ₹1,000 if your total income is up to ₹5 lakh, ₹5,000 above that. It applies from the day after your due date, regardless of whether you owe any tax.

Section 234A is interest at 1% per month or part month on unpaid tax. "Part month" is doing real work in that sentence — filing on 1 August when your deadline was 31 July triggers a full month's interest, not one day's. If you owe ₹80,000 and file five weeks late, that is two months of interest at 1%, so ₹1,600, plus the ₹5,000 fee.

The cost that is not a penalty: file after your due date and you lose the right to carry forward losses — business losses, capital losses, speculation losses. Only house property loss survives a belated return. For anyone who had a bad year in equities, this is far more expensive than the ₹5,000 fee, and it is irreversible. A capital loss you cannot carry forward is a deduction against future gains you will never get back.

The regime default still trips people up

The new regime is the default. If you file without actively selecting the old regime, you are taxed under the new one — no HRA exemption, no 80C, no 80D, but a ₹75,000 standard deduction and lower slab rates.

For salaried filers the choice can be made afresh each year at filing. For business and professional income it cannot: opting out of the new regime is done via Form 10-IEA, and switching back is once in a lifetime. That asymmetry is worth pausing on before you tick a box in a hurry on 31 July.

What to do in the next two days

  • Confirm which bucket you are in. Not liable to audit and filing ITR-1 or ITR-2 means Friday. Not liable to audit but filing ITR-3 or ITR-4 means you have another month.
  • Reconcile AIS and Form 26AS before you file, not after. Mismatches are the most common trigger for a notice, and they are trivial to fix beforehand and tedious to fix afterwards.
  • If you are going to be late anyway, still file by 31 December to stay within the belated window — but understand you have already lost loss carry-forward by then.
  • E-verify within 30 days. An unverified return is treated as never filed, which is a way to incur every late-filing consequence while believing you have complied.

What is the ITR filing last date for AY 2026-27?

It depends on your audit position, not your form. Salaried individuals and HUFs filing ITR-1 or ITR-2 must file by 31 July 2026. Those filing ITR-3 or ITR-4 whose accounts are not liable to audit under Section 44AB have until 31 August 2026 — a permanent change made by the Finance Act 2026 amending Section 139(1). Cases liable to audit file by 31 October 2026, and transfer pricing cases by 30 November 2026.

Why do ITR-3 and ITR-4 filers get an extra month?

The Finance Act 2026 amended Section 139(1) to give non-audit business and professional filers until 31 August, recognising that they need time to close books and reconcile accounts that a salaried filer does not. It is a statutory amendment effective from AY 2026-27 onwards, not the kind of one-off extension the department used to grant by circular in late July. Salaried filers were not included and remain on 31 July.

Will the 31 July deadline be extended this year?

The extension already happened, in a sense — it was legislated, and it applied only to non-audit ITR-3 and ITR-4 filers. Because the split is now written into Section 139(1) rather than granted administratively, there is less reason to expect a further circular for salaried filers. Planning around a hoped-for extension is a poor strategy when the fee for being wrong is ₹5,000 plus interest plus the loss of loss carry-forward.

What is the penalty for filing ITR after the due date?

A late fee under Section 234F of ₹1,000 if your total income is up to ₹5 lakh and ₹5,000 above that, plus interest under Section 234A at 1% per month or part month on any unpaid tax. Note that "part month" means filing one day late still costs a full month of interest. The larger cost is usually non-monetary: filing after the due date forfeits the right to carry forward business, capital and speculation losses, with only house property loss surviving.

Can I revise my return if I make a mistake?

Yes, and the window is longer than it used to be. Revised returns under Section 139(5) can now be filed up to 31 March 2027 for AY 2026-27, extended from the earlier 31 December cut-off. Beyond that, an updated return under ITR-U is available for up to 48 months from the end of the assessment year, but it carries additional tax. The revised-return route is far cheaper, which is why the extra three months is worth knowing about.

Which tax regime applies if I do not choose one?

The new regime, which is the default. You get a ₹75,000 standard deduction and lower slab rates but forgo HRA, 80C, 80D and most other deductions. Salaried taxpayers can choose afresh each year when filing. Those with business or professional income must opt out using Form 10-IEA, and switching back to the new regime afterwards is permitted only once in a lifetime — so the choice deserves more care than a tick-box on deadline day.
