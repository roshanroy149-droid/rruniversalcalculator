How are macro targets calculated?

Your BMR is estimated with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, multiplied by an activity factor to get TDEE (maintenance calories), then adjusted by ±500 kcal/day for a lose/gain goal. The resulting target calories are split by percentage into protein and carbs (4 kcal/gram each) and fat (9 kcal/gram).

Which diet style should I pick?

Balanced (30/40/30) suits most people as a default. High-protein favors muscle retention during a calorie deficit. Low-carb and keto are stricter approaches some people find helpful for appetite control — none of these splits is universally "correct"; the best one is whichever you can sustain.

Is this medical or dietary advice?

No — this is a population-average formula estimate, the same one used by the Calorie / BMR Calculator. Individual needs vary with body composition, medical conditions, and activity in ways a generic formula can't capture. Consult a doctor or registered dietitian before making significant dietary changes.

What the split actually does

Total calories decide whether you gain or lose weight. Macros decide the composition of that change — how much of a loss is fat rather than muscle, and how much of a gain is muscle rather than fat. Both matter, but not for the same thing.

The energy values are fixed: protein and carbohydrate supply 4 kcal per gram, fat 9. So a 2,000 kcal day split 40/30/30 works out to 200g carbohydrate, 150g protein and 66.7g fat. That asymmetry is why fat grams always look small next to carb grams — fat is more than twice as energy-dense.

Protein is the one worth getting right

If you only control one macro, control protein. It preserves lean mass in a deficit, has the highest thermic effect — roughly 20-30% of its energy is spent digesting it — and is the most satiating of the three per calorie.

Common targets run from about 1.6g to 2.2g per kg of bodyweight for people training regularly, with the upper end more relevant when in a deficit. The Protein Calculator works this out against your goal.

Carbohydrate and fat are more flexible than they're presented

Once protein and total calories are set, the carb/fat split is largely preference. Higher carbohydrate tends to suit high training volume and endurance work; higher fat suits people who find it more satiating or prefer fewer, larger meals. Fat should not go below roughly 0.5g per kg of bodyweight, as it is needed for hormone production and for absorbing fat-soluble vitamins.

Most of the popular diet arguments are about this split, and the evidence for one being universally superior at matched calories and protein is weak.

Practical notes

  • Hitting protein and calories reliably beats hitting all three imperfectly. The other two have room to move.
  • Fibre sits inside your carbohydrate number and is worth tracking separately — 25-30g a day is a reasonable target.
  • Alcohol is a fourth macro at 7 kcal/g and is usually left out of splits entirely, which quietly breaks the arithmetic.
  • Recalculate after meaningful weight change. Targets set at a different bodyweight drift out of date.
