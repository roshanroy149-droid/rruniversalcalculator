Two verdicts, from the same numbers, that can disagree

This page’s own defaults show why refinancing needs two separate questions asked, not one. A $280,000 balance at 6.5% with 25 years remaining, refinanced into a new 30-year loan at 5.5%, with $4,000 in closing costs:

  • Current payment: $1,891/mo  →  New payment: $1,590/mo — a saving of $301 every month.
  • Break-even on the $4,000 closing cost: just 14 months.
  • Total interest, current loan (25 years left): $287,174. Total interest, new loan (full 30-year term): $292,331 — $5,157 more.

The break-even calculation, taken alone, says this refinance is a clear win — the fees pay for themselves in barely over a year. The total-interest column says the opposite: this loan costs more over its life. Both are correct. The break-even math only asks when the monthly savings offset the upfront fee; it has no opinion about the length of the loan. Resetting to a fresh 30-year term after 25 years is what does the damage — you gain five years of low payments and pay for them with five extra years of interest on the back end, even at a meaningfully lower rate.

The fix that keeps the savings without the extra years

Refinance into a term that matches what is actually left, not the lender's default term. Choosing a new 25-year loan here instead of 30, at the same 5.5%, changes the trade entirely: the payment comes to $1,719 rather than $1,590, so the monthly saving shrinks to $171 instead of $301 — but total interest falls to $235,833, which is $51,341 less than the current loan's $287,174, not $5,157 more. A smaller monthly saving paired with a genuine six-figure reduction in lifetime interest, instead of a larger monthly saving that quietly costs more overall. Whenever a refinance offer defaults to a fresh 30-year term, ask what the shorter option costs before assuming the bigger headline saving is the better deal.

A lower rate does not automatically mean a better refinance

Three things can each independently make a lower-rate refinance a worse deal in total: resetting the amortization clock (as above), closing costs large enough that you move before break-even, and a rate drop too small to justify the fees at all. A refinance is only unambiguously good when the new rate is meaningfully lower, the new term does not stretch materially past your original one, and you are confident you will stay in the loan well past the break-even point. Miss any one of the three and "lower rate" stops being the same thing as "better deal."

How is the break-even point calculated?

Break-even months = closing costs ÷ monthly savings. If refinancing saves you $200/month and costs $4,000 in closing costs, you break even in 20 months — refinancing is worth it financially if you plan to stay in the home (or keep the loan) longer than that.

Does a lower rate always mean a better refinance?

Not necessarily — resetting to a new 30-year term after you've already paid down several years of your current loan can lower your monthly payment while increasing total interest paid, since you restart the amortization clock at year zero. Always compare total remaining interest, not just the monthly payment, using the figures above.

What counts as closing costs?

Typically 2–5% of the loan amount, covering appraisal, origination fees, title insurance, and other lender and third-party charges. Get an actual Loan Estimate from your lender rather than assuming a flat percentage — costs vary meaningfully by lender and region.

Worked example: a $280,000 balance at 6.5% with 25 years remaining costs about $1,891/month. Refinancing into a new 30-year loan at 5.5% costs about $1,590/month — a $301/month saving. Against $4,000 in closing costs, that's a break-even of about 14 months.

Can I refinance a federal student loan?

One refinance is not reversible in the way the others are: refinancing a federal student loan with a private lender converts it permanently to private terms, giving up income-driven repayment, Public Service Loan Forgiveness and federal deferment rights for good. A lower rate can still be worth it, but price what you would be surrendering first — the income-driven repayment calculator shows what the federal plans would have cost over the same period.
