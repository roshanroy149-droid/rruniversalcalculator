What is TDEE, and how is it different from BMR?

BMR (basal metabolic rate) is what your body burns at complete rest just to stay alive — breathing, circulation, cell repair. TDEE (total daily energy expenditure) is BMR multiplied by an activity factor, so it also covers walking around, working, and training. BMR is the floor; TDEE is the realistic daily total, and it's the number you actually plan calorie intake against.

Which activity level should I pick?

Sedentary (×1.2) is a desk job with little deliberate exercise. Lightly active (×1.375) is light exercise 1–3 days a week. Moderately active (×1.55) is moderate exercise 3–5 days a week. Very active (×1.725) is hard exercise 6–7 days a week. Athlete (×1.9) is twice-daily training or physical labour on top of training. Most people overestimate — if you're unsure between two levels, the lower one is usually closer.

Which formula does this use?

The Mifflin-St Jeor equation, which has generally outperformed the older Harris-Benedict equation in validation studies against measured resting energy expenditure. For men it's (10 × kg) + (6.25 × cm) − (5 × age) + 5; for women the final term is −161 instead of +5. That BMR is then multiplied by your activity factor to reach TDEE.

How accurate is a TDEE calculation?

It's a population-average estimate, typically within about 10% for most people but capable of being further off at the extremes of body composition. The equation doesn't know your muscle mass, and the activity multiplier is a broad band rather than a measurement. Treat the number as a starting point: track your weight over 2–3 weeks and adjust up or down based on what actually happens on the scale.

How many calories should I eat to lose or gain weight?

A deficit of roughly 15–25% below TDEE is a common cutting range, and a surplus of about 10–20% above TDEE is a common lean-bulking range — both shown above. Since roughly 7,700 calories is often cited as equating to one kilogram of body mass, a 500-calorie daily deficit works out to somewhere near 0.45 kg a week in theory, though real-world results vary with water weight, adherence, and metabolic adaptation.

Worked example: a 30-year-old man, 80 kg and 180 cm, has a BMR of (10×80) + (6.25×180) − (5×30) + 5 = 1,780 calories. At the moderately active multiplier of ×1.55 that's a TDEE of about 2,759 calories — so roughly 979 calories a day come from activity rather than resting metabolism. A standard 20% cut puts him near 2,207 a day, and a 15% lean bulk near 3,173.
