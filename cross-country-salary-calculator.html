The mistake almost every salary comparison makes

Pension contributions are not tax, and treating them as the same thing produces badly wrong answers.

An employee in Singapore under 55 contributes 20% of wages to CPF. That looks brutal next to the UAE's zero. But CPF money is yours — it sits in your account, earns interest, and funds your retirement and housing. India's EPF works the same way. UK National Insurance and US FICA do not: they fund current benefits, and while they build entitlement, they don't accrue to a personal balance you can point at.

So a country that deducts 30% where 20 points of it are your own retirement savings is in a completely different position from one that deducts 30% in pure tax. This calculator labels which is which underneath each result, rather than summing them into a single misleading "total deductions" number.

Why the headline number moves so much

Three things drive most of the gap between countries:

  • Where the brackets start. The UK's personal allowance is £12,570 and Australia's tax-free threshold is A$18,200, but India's new regime pays nothing up to ₹12 lakh of taxable income once the Section 87A rebate applies. Thresholds matter more than headline rates at middle incomes.
  • Whether contributions are capped. US Social Security stops at $184,500 of wages and Canada's CPP at $85,000, so the effective rate falls as high earners cross those ceilings. Germany's ceilings work the same way. The UK is unusual in charging 2% with no upper limit.
  • What the state provides in return. Germany's ~19.7% employee share includes health insurance. A US figure that looks lighter often isn't, once employer-plan premiums and deductibles are counted — and those don't appear on a payslip as tax.

What this deliberately leaves out

Sub-national tax. US state income tax ranges from nothing in Texas and Florida to over 13% at the top in California. Canadian provincial tax roughly doubles the federal figure in some provinces. Germany adds church tax where applicable. These are large enough to flip a comparison, so treat the US, Canada and Germany numbers as a federal-level floor.

Cost of living. Converting currency is arithmetic and can be done exactly; adjusting for cost of living cannot. It depends on the city, on whether you rent or own, on schooling, and on how you actually spend. Any single multiplier would be a guess wearing the costume of a fact. Use the converted figure as the starting point, then price rent, healthcare and schooling for the specific city.

The things that aren't salary. Employer pension matching, equity, healthcare, notice periods, visa portability and paid leave routinely outweigh a 10% difference in gross pay. A US offer with a 6% 401(k) match and one without are not the same offer.

A note on the exchange rate

Conversion uses live mid-market rates — the rate quoted in the news, and the one you will never quite get. An actual transfer typically costs 0.5% to 3% more once the spread and fees are included, which the FX Fee Calculator will quantify for a specific provider. If you are moving money regularly, that gap compounds into real numbers.

Rates also move. A comparison that looks close today can look different in six months, which is an argument for weighing the structural differences — the brackets, the ceilings, what healthcare costs you — more heavily than the converted total.
