// THE TALLYBENCH BOOKS

Two books. One method. Pick the one that matches your tax system.

Both teach personal finance the same way — every concept attached to arithmetic you can reproduce, and a free calculator on this site for every chapter. What differs is the country: one is written for the US, the other for India, and the rules are different enough that reading the wrong one would waste your time.

Venros · First Edition, 2026

From Paycheck to Portfolio — United States

Get it on Amazon →

A Numbers-First Guide to Personal Finance. ~80 pages. Paperback ISBN 979-8-18895-193-1, also on Kindle. Pricing varies by marketplace.

Most personal finance writing has the same problem: it tells you what to do without ever showing the arithmetic. You're told to build an emergency fund, avoid bad debt, start investing early — and left to work out what any of that means for your actual salary.

This takes the opposite approach. Every concept arrives attached to a calculation you can reproduce, and every calculation has a free tool on this site that runs it for your own numbers.

It also follows three people all the way through. Raj, Maya and Priya appear in Part 1 with a payslip and a bank balance, and are still there in Part 5 with a retirement target — the continuity a single calculator genuinely cannot give you.

Paisa Playbook — India

Get it on Amazon.in →

A Numbers-First Guide to Money in India. 91 pages. For salaried professionals and freelancers. Available on Kindle; also on Amazon.com, with pricing varying by marketplace.

Real rupees. Real rules. Every number worked out.

Generic personal finance advice fails in India for a specific reason: the accounts are different. A guide built around 401(k)s and Roth IRAs cannot tell you whether to pick the new regime or the old, what your CTC actually converts to in-hand, or why the same mutual fund quietly costs you more in a regular plan than a direct one.

So this isn't a translation of the US book — it shares the five-part skeleton and the worked-example method, but the content is rewritten around Indian rules. It also covers freelancers properly, which most Indian personal finance writing skips entirely: advance tax, presumptive taxation under 44ADA, and when GST registration starts to apply.

Which one is for you

Pick by tax system, not by preference. The method is identical; the rules are not, and the mismatch is not cosmetic. A US reader has no use for 44ADA, and an Indian reader gains nothing from a chapter on Roth conversions.

  • File taxes in India → Paisa Playbook. CTC, CIBIL, EPF/PPF/NPS, the regime choice, GST for freelancers.
  • File taxes in the US → From Paycheck to Portfolio. 401(k), IRA, credit scores, federal brackets.
  • Neither? The two universal parts — compounding and debt payoff arithmetic — work anywhere, but the tax chapters won't apply. Both books say so plainly rather than pretending to be global.

Both are written for someone who has income and wants to understand what to do with it, but has never been shown the mechanics. Neither is for you if you already run a diversified portfolio and rebalance annually, or if you're looking for stock picks — and neither is a tax filing manual. They explain how the systems work; they don't walk you through a return.

An honest note on the numbers

The arithmetic is real and reproducible. Take any worked example and check it against the calculator on this site — that's rather the point of publishing them together.

The people are not real. Both books say so in their own disclaimers: Raj, Maya and Priya, and every amount attached to them, are illustrative and fictional, built to demonstrate a method rather than to represent current rates or promise an outcome. Paisa Playbook goes a step further with an appendix — Figures That Change, and Where to Check Them — listing which numbers date fastest and the official source for each.

And to be direct about what this page is: both books are paid products published by Venros, which also builds TallyBench. The calculators stay free, with no sign-up, exactly as they are. Nothing in either book is required to use them.

Which of the two books should I read?

Pick by tax system, not by preference. If you file taxes in India, read Paisa Playbook — it covers CTC versus in-hand pay, the new versus old regime choice, CIBIL scores, EPF/PPF/NPS, and freelance rules like advance tax, 44ADA and GST. If you file in the US, read From Paycheck to Portfolio, which covers 401(k)s, IRAs, credit scores and US tax brackets. The method is the same in both; the rules, accounts and account names are entirely different.

Is Paisa Playbook a translation of the other book?

No. It shares the same five-part structure and the same worked-example approach, but the content is rewritten for India rather than translated. Whole chapters exist only in the India edition — CTC versus in-hand pay, how a CIBIL score is built, direct versus regular mutual fund plans, KYC and demat account setup, and a full freelance section on advance tax, 44ADA and GST.

Are the books just the free calculators in written form?

No. The calculators answer a single question each; the books explain which question to ask, in what order, and what the answer means. They also run three case studies end to end — Raj, Maya and Priya — where one person's numbers carry through budgeting, debt payoff, investing and retirement, which is something no individual calculator can do.

Are the numbers in the books real?

The arithmetic is real and reproducible — you can check any worked example against the calculators on this site. The people and scenarios are not. Both books state plainly that Raj, Maya and Priya, and all the amounts attached to them, are illustrative and fictional, created to demonstrate a method rather than to represent current rates or guaranteed outcomes. Paisa Playbook goes further and includes an appendix listing which figures change over time and where to check the current ones.

Is this financial advice?

No. Both books are educational and carry an explicit disclaimer. Interest rates, tax slabs, contribution limits and account rules change over time and vary by country, so they tell you to verify current figures against an official source before acting, and to speak to a licensed professional about your own circumstances.
